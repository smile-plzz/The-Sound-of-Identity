# The Sound of Identity: Music, Cultural Geography, Generational Change, and Social Behavior
## A Global Comparative Review with Bangladesh as a Case Study

**Status:** Scholarly narrative-review draft, 12 October 2026. **Not peer reviewed; no original participant data.**

### Abstract
Music both reflects and participates in the construction of identity, place, generational memory, and social belonging. This exploratory interdisciplinary narrative review connects scholarship on everyday listening, rock and pop scenes, local/global cultural circulation, musical reminiscence, and associations between musical preferences and substance use. Bangladesh is considered as a contextual case of Bangla-language popular music and folk–popular hybridity. The literature suggests that musical preferences are socially situated rather than intrinsic markers of personality or behavior. Peer contexts are especially important when interpreting the association between genre preferences and substance use; causal claims are not justified by the observational studies reviewed. The article proposes a five-domain framework linking geographical context, genre/scene participation, generation, peer networks, and media distribution, and outlines methods for future culturally grounded research. Because the search was exploratory and not exhaustive, this synthesis neither claims systematic coverage nor reports new empirical findings.

**Keywords:** music sociology; identity; culture; geographical place; rock; pop; Bangladesh; musical memory; peer influence; substance use

## 1. Introduction
Musical taste can signal belonging, carry intergenerational memory, and express relationships to particular places. At the same time, people encounter music through institutions, families, peer groups, markets, and media technologies. Music's cultural role is therefore reciprocal: individuals and communities make music meaningful, and musical practices give them resources to construct meaning. DeNora (2000) conceptualizes music as active in everyday social life. North and Hargreaves (1999) examine how adolescents use music-related preferences as social identity markers.

The international circulation of rock and pop provides a useful comparative lens precisely because these genre labels are historically and geographically variable. Imported styles can be adopted, translated, contested, and reshaped by local languages and traditions. Bangladesh presents a case through which to examine the interaction between popular band music, folk inheritance, and global forms (Yun, 2019). We ask: How does music participate in cultural and geographical identity? What distinctions between rock and pop are analytically useful? How do generations transmit musical meaning? What, if anything, can observational research establish about musical taste and substance use?

## 2. Review approach
This is a structured exploratory *narrative review*, not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Initial searches identified scholarly works through publisher pages, academic indexes, bibliographic records, and repositories, emphasizing identity, youth scenes, musical taste, cultural geography, Bangladesh, memory, and substance use. The methods and source register are recorded in `research/search-protocol.md` and `research/evidence-register.csv`. Some sources were examined at abstract/metadata level; not all full texts were retrieved. There was no exhaustive screening, independent reviewer process, or formal quality appraisal. Interpretations are therefore provisional, and numerical synthesis or claims of comprehensive coverage would be unwarranted.

## 3. Music and the formation of identity
DeNora (2000) treats music as a resource through which people organize mood, action, and self-understanding rather than as a passive mirror of culture. Musical preferences can also become visible badges of group affiliation (North & Hargreaves, 1999). Rentfrow and Gosling (2003) report patterned associations between musical preference and individual differences across several studies; these correlations do not imply that genres cause fixed personalities.

At least three pathways need to be distinguished. **Selection:** listeners may choose music that is compatible with existing values, biographies, and peer environments. **Expression:** listening and participation communicate social affiliation. **Reinforcement:** musical scenes sustain friendships, routines, symbolic boundaries, and shared meaning. Because these pathways may occur together, cross-sectional correlations cannot cleanly isolate them.

## 4. Cultural geography and localization
A locality influences access to instruments, performance spaces, languages, historical narratives, and media systems. Yet musical forms circulate internationally. The interpretation of rock, pop, and folk fusion should therefore attend to *localization* rather than assign fixed geographical ownership. Yun (2019) discusses hybridity in connection with the Bangladeshi fusion band Bangla, offering an entry point to investigate the encounter between local folk-derived aesthetics and contemporary popular formats.

The history of Bengali-language rock also extends across state boundaries. Scholarship on Kolkata's Moheener Ghoraguli provides a regional comparison, but Kolkata's Indian setting must not be conflated with Bangladesh. Musical exchange can occur across borders without producing a single homogeneous Bengali music culture. Platform-mediated circulation adds further complexities: global visibility does not guarantee equality of access, interpretation, or local participation.

## 5. Rock, pop, and cultural scenes
Rock and pop are neither mutually exclusive nor stable worldwide categories. They can describe sounds, commercial infrastructures, performance conventions, shared identities, and marketing labels. It would be methodologically unsound to assume that rock audiences are inherently rebellious or substance-oriented, or that pop audiences are conventional or politically disengaged. Bennett (1999) argues that youth musical affiliation may be fluid and shifting rather than confined to rigid subcultures.

Comparison should therefore use observable and locally interpreted phenomena: scenes, venues, language choice, fashion, concert participation, digital communities, musical production, social networks, and listeners' own descriptions. One person may belong to multiple scenes or engage with different genres in different settings. Bangladesh-focused work could compare university concerts, commercial pop channels, independent rehearsals, folk-fusion performances, and online communities without assuming that a genre label has identical implications everywhere.

## 6. Age, generation, and memory
Krumhansl and Zupnick (2013) describe cascading reminiscence effects associated with popular music, including exposure transmitted from older generations. Musical nostalgia may reflect socialization in childhood and adolescence, family listening habits, and historical media environments. A strong generational analysis must distinguish **age effects** (life-course change), **cohort effects** (shared formative experiences), and **period effects** (events or technologies affecting many ages). A single cross-sectional survey cannot uniquely separate them. Future work should measure birth year, formative listening, family influences, migration, streaming habits, and autobiographical memory while considering retrospective reporting bias.

## 7. Music, peer groups, and substance use
Some observational studies report associations among musical preferences, smoking, drinking, and other substance-related behaviors. Mulder and colleagues (2009) examine such genre-linked patterns among Dutch adolescents. In a sample of 7,324 Dutch students aged 12–16, Mulder and colleagues (2010) report that perceived peer substance use partially or fully mediates certain relationships between music preferences and substance use. That evidence does *not* establish that musical exposure causes drug consumption.

Selection into peer networks, sensation seeking, socioeconomic position, family background, and opportunities for substance access can confound observed relationships. Nor should tobacco, alcohol, and cannabis be treated as an interchangeable outcome. Genre labels and social norms also differ between societies. Responsible Bangladeshi research would require ethical review, confidential protocols, culturally appropriate wording, and avoidance of stigmatizing labels.

A more testable question than “Does rock cause drug use?” is: **How much of any observed association between musical-scene participation and substance-use attitudes is accounted for by peer networks and relevant social variables?** Any answer would still depend on study design and its ability to support causal inference.

## 8. Integrative framework and provisional gap
We propose five interacting analytical dimensions: **geography/place** (language, locality, migration and institutions); **genre/scene** (performance, aesthetics and group meanings); **generation/biography** (formative exposure and memory); **peers/social relations** (belonging and behavioral environments); and **media/distribution** (radio, television, concerts, streaming and social platforms). The dimensions are not a validated causal model. Their value is to organize evidence and future comparisons.

The selected literature examines several of these domains separately. It does not yet demonstrate a directly comparable, integrated account across global settings with Bangladesh-specific validation. This is a **gap identified in the present source sample**, not proof that no prior integrated study exists. Citation chaining and a broader systematic search must test the novelty claim.

Potential *future* hypotheses include: H1, stronger scene participation correlates with stronger scene-specific social identification; H2, formative musical exposure predicts nostalgia beyond present-day preferences; H3, peer variables attenuate correlations between genre preference and substance attitudes; H4, local-language attachment and musical experience condition meanings ascribed to hybrid genres. These are proposals, **not empirical findings of this manuscript**.

## 9. Empirical research agenda
A sequential mixed-methods program could begin with ethnographic mapping and interviews across contrasting Bangladeshi musical settings, followed by a culturally adapted adult survey. Survey instruments should distinguish liking, listening frequency, active scene participation, identity salience, peer exposure, and behavioral outcomes. Any numerical sample goal must be justified by power or precision analysis; no data have yet been collected. International comparison requires translation and measurement-equivalence testing. Recruitments involving sensitive behavioral topics require appropriate ethics oversight and privacy safeguards, particularly for minors.

## 10. Discussion and conclusion
Taken together, these studies support a relational interpretation of music and culture. Listeners use music to express affiliations and organize memory, but taste and participation are also shaped by geography, biography, networks, and technological access. Bangladeshi folk–popular fusion illustrates the need to study the negotiation of local and global forms in their specific historical context. Rock and pop should be examined as evolving cultural scenes rather than fixed behavioral or personality types. Research on substance use particularly cautions against attributing complex social behaviors to musical genres when peers and other factors may explain observed associations.

The present review is limited by a non-exhaustive search, partial full-text access, and the initial evidence set's Western emphasis. Stronger Bangladesh-specific sampling and a systematic scholarly search are necessary before making nationwide or causal generalizations. The next contribution should be a documented scoping review and an ethically sound, culturally valid empirical study.
