# The Sound of Identity: Music, Geography, Generations, and Social Behavior in Global Comparison with Bangladesh

## Stage 1 research foundation

**Document type:** Structured evidence review and research design brief  
**Date:** 11 October 2026  
**Status:** Research stage complete; manuscript drafting not yet begun  

## 1. Scope and epistemic position

This project asks how music both reflects and participates in the formation of cultural and geographical identity, how rock and pop organize different kinds of affiliation, how these processes vary by age and generation, and how peer groups and substance use enter the same social field. Bangladesh is treated as a theoretically meaningful case rather than as an illustrative afterthought.

The defensible claim is not that music mechanically causes identity or substance use. Music is better understood as a cultural resource, symbolic marker, social setting, and medium through which people classify themselves and others. In the behavioral literature, musical preferences, peer networks, media exposure, and substance use are associated in patterned ways, but selection, socialization, shared background, personality, family context, and commercial media exposure are difficult to separate. The review therefore distinguishes association, mediation, interpretation, and causal effect throughout.

## 2. Review form and search strategy

### 2.1 Review design

This is a **structured integrative review**, not a completed PRISMA systematic review or meta-analysis. It combines cultural sociology, popular-music studies, geography, developmental psychology, communication, and public-health research. The heterogeneous questions, constructs, and designs make statistical pooling inappropriate. The review is systematic in recording searches and verification, but it does not claim exhaustive database coverage, independent dual screening, preregistration, or a complete unpublished-literature search.

### 2.2 Search date and discovery channels

Searches were conducted on 11 October 2026 using web-indexed academic discovery, publisher pages, DOI landing pages, PubMed/PMC, institutional repositories, and journal sites. DOI metadata were cross-checked against publisher or indexing records when available. Sources whose existence or metadata could not be verified were excluded from the citable corpus.

### 2.3 Search strings

Searches used combinations of the following strings:

### 2.4 Eligibility criteria

Included sources had to meet at least one of these functions: establish a theoretical mechanism; present primary empirical evidence on music, identity, place, age, peer relations, or substance-related behavior; offer a comparative non-Bangladesh case; or provide Bangladesh-specific empirical or historical analysis. Peer-reviewed journal articles and scholarly books were prioritized. Recent Bangladesh-specific articles were retained when they directly addressed the case and had verifiable metadata.

Excluded materials included Wikipedia, Reddit, general journalism, unverified manuscripts, sources with unresolved metadata, and studies whose relevance was only incidental. News and community discussions may later inform contextual sampling but are not evidence for manuscript claims.

### 2.5 Screening and verification record

The discovery set contained more than 35 potentially relevant records. Twenty-one high-relevance scholarly works were retained in the core evidence map below. Verification means that the title, authorship or publisher record, venue, and DOI or stable scholarly identifier were confirmed. It does not mean that every article was available in full text. Claims drawn from abstract-only records are kept at the level supported by the abstract.

## 3. Theoretical framework

The paper should use an integrated framework rather than treating “music influences culture” as a single mechanism.

### 3.1 Music as identity work

Music supplies repertoires for self-narration, emotion regulation, impression management, and belonging. Social identity approaches explain how taste can operate as a badge that marks in-groups and out-groups. Developmental work adds that adolescence is a particularly important period because identity exploration, peer affiliation, and taste formation coincide. This does not make identity fixed: people shift genres, combine affiliations, and reinterpret earlier music across the life course.

### 3.2 Scenes, place, and scale

Scene theory replaces the assumption that a genre maps neatly onto a single bounded subculture. Musical life can be local, translocal, and virtual, with venues, media, technologies, audiences, and symbolic boundaries jointly producing a sense of place. A scene may represent a city or nation while relying on global genres and online circulation. The key geographical question is therefore not whether music is “local” or “global,” but how locality is produced through their interaction.

### 3.3 Glocalization and cultural hybridity

Studies of Hong Kong, Singapore, diaspora, and Bangladesh show that global forms are localized through language, history, class, performance conventions, and political meanings. Hybridization is not cultural dilution by definition. It can be an active way of making a global form locally intelligible, though unequal media industries and platform visibility shape whose hybrid forms circulate.

### 3.4 Subculture, post-subculture, and genre boundary work

Classic subculture models emphasize coherent style, resistance, and social location. Later scene and post-subcultural approaches emphasize fluid membership, overlapping tastes, and translocal networks. Rock and metal often organize authenticity around musicianship, non-mainstream status, live performance, and resistance. Pop tends to be more explicitly mediated through charts, stars, visual performance, fandom, and commercial circulation. These are empirical tendencies, not intrinsic genre essences. Rock can be commercial and pop can support oppositional identities.

### 3.5 Reinforcing spirals, selection, and socialization

The behavioral portion should use a reciprocal model. Young people may select peers with similar tastes and behaviors; peers may then reinforce preferences and norms; media choices may reflect prior dispositions while also increasing exposure to particular representations. Longitudinal work supports mediation and reciprocal pathways more than a one-way “music causes behavior” thesis.

### 3.6 Generation, cohort, and life course

Age effects must be separated from cohort effects. Adolescence often carries heightened musical importance, but generational identity depends on historically specific media systems, political events, technologies, and formative repertoires. Nostalgia and cultural memory can keep a scene meaningful after its commercial peak. Cross-sectional age differences cannot by themselves demonstrate generational change.

## 4. Evidence synthesis

### 4.1 Music reflects place while helping to produce it

Research on scenes and cultural geography treats place identity as relational and performed. Peterson and Bennett’s local/translocal/virtual distinction shows that geographic identity is not erased by global circulation. Ho’s study of Hong Kong and Kong’s study of Singapore both show how international forms become local through language, institutions, production, and audience interpretation. Research on Sheffield demonstrates that popular music can be folded into urban memory and tourism, while Bennett and Rogers show how scenes survive through cultural memory and emotional geography.

The synthesis is that music does not merely represent a pre-existing geography. Venues, festivals, media infrastructures, local histories, accents, languages, and remembered events make places audible and socially meaningful. Digital circulation extends rather than automatically dissolves those attachments.

### 4.2 Music is a resource for identity, not a deterministic mold

Developmental and social-psychological studies consistently show that adolescents use music for enjoyment, mood, self-definition, social image, and affiliation. Friendship research found that similarity in musical preferences predicted the formation of new friendships, but not the ending of existing ones. Network research likewise supports music as a marker of friendship selection. These results fit a homophily account: taste helps people recognize compatible others.

The literature also warns against treating every listener as equally invested. Genre preferences do not map perfectly onto personality or conduct, and private tastes may diverge from shared peer-group tastes. Music enables identity work, but family, class, gender, ethnicity, religion, education, and platform access condition that work.

### 4.3 Rock and pop organize different, overlapping cultural logics

Rock and metal studies frequently foreground authenticity, live participation, technical competence, underground status, masculine coding, and resistance to a mainstream. Pop studies more often foreground stars, visuality, dance, charts, transnational media, fandom, and commercial accessibility. The opposition is unstable. Quader and Redden’s Bangladesh study is especially useful: Dhaka metal participants distinguished themselves from mainstream rock and pop, yet many did not regard commercial success as betrayal. The scene was translocal and alternative, but participation depended on middle-class resources and was heavily male.

Bangladesh therefore complicates imported subculture models. “Underground” can signify experimentation, alternative space, and critique without implying a unified anti-capitalist politics. Likewise, K-pop fandom among Bangladeshi young adults illustrates that pop affiliation may create active online communities and transnational cultural learning rather than passive mass consumption.

### 4.4 Age, generation, and memory

Adolescence is repeatedly identified as a period when music becomes tightly linked to peers, possible selves, and emotion. Longitudinal and life-course scholarship suggests that formative-period music can retain unusual salience, but generational claims require historical evidence. A person being older and preferring older music does not reveal whether the cause is aging, cohort membership, repeated exposure, or memory.

For Bangladesh, likely generational anchors include the Language Movement, the Liberation War, post-independence band music, the 1990s and 2000s rock/metal scenes, satellite television, MP3 and piracy cultures, YouTube, streaming, and contemporary transnational fandom. These should be studied as hypotheses and periodizing contexts, not assumed as uniform experiences.

### 4.5 Peer groups, media exposure, and substance use

The strongest conclusion is an association embedded in peer and media systems, not direct musical causation. Mulder and colleagues found that perceived peer substance use wholly or partly mediated associations between music-style preferences and adolescent smoking, alcohol use, and cannabis use in a large Dutch school sample. Slater and Henry’s prospective study likewise modeled peer-group mediation between music-related media exposure and substance-use initiation. Selfhout and colleagues show why selection matters: musical similarity helps structure new friendships.

Content analyses demonstrate that alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, and other drugs appear in popular music, often positively and unevenly by genre. Such studies establish exposure opportunities, not behavioral effects. Cross-sectional associations can reflect self-selection, shared peer norms, sensation seeking, socioeconomic conditions, family factors, nightlife participation, or other unmeasured influences. Even longitudinal mediation does not eliminate all confounding.

The paper should therefore avoid phrases such as “rock causes drug use” or “pop protects young people.” Genre categories vary across countries and periods, and the behavioral meanings of a scene depend on its local institutions and peer practices. Bangladesh-specific evidence connecting musical affiliation with substance use is presently too thin for substantive causal or prevalence claims.

### 4.6 Bangladesh as a case of layered sonic identity

Bangladesh connects music to language, nationalism, secular and religious contestation, liberation memory, urban youth scenes, class, and globalization. Work on Language Movement songs and Rabindrasangīt demonstrates how music participates in linguistic nationalism and debates over Bengali, Bangladeshi, Muslim, and syncretic identities. These are not merely heritage forms: their public meanings remain politically contested.

Quader and Redden provide the strongest directly relevant empirical study of contemporary genre culture. Based on 14 interviews, observations at ten gigs, and textual materials, they describe a Dhaka-centered metal scene shaped by global genres, local frustration, alternative-space making, and unequal material access. The narrow, predominantly male and middle-class sample limits generalization, but the study gives a concrete mechanism through which global rock forms acquire local meaning.

Mukherjee’s study of Bangla rock develops the counterculture and dissidence theme across postcolonial Bengali popular music, while recent work on Bangladeshi K-pop fandom shows how digital, transnational pop affiliations expand the field beyond a rock/pop binary. Together, these sources suggest that Bangladesh is best treated as a layered ecology: nationalist and heritage repertoires coexist and compete with band music, metal, film music, global pop, and digitally organized fandoms.

## 5. Research gaps

1. **Bangladesh lacks a broad, contemporary, multi-method map of musical identity.** Existing work is valuable but fragmented by genre, city, period, or historical repertoire.
2. **Rock receives richer subcultural analysis than mainstream pop in Bangladesh.** Direct comparison of their identity practices, spaces, class composition, gender dynamics, and moral boundaries is missing.
3. **The Dhaka bias is substantial.** Secondary cities, peri-urban areas, rural youth, and diasporic Bangladeshis are insufficiently represented.
4. **Generational language is often asserted rather than designed.** There is little work separating age, cohort, period, and life-course effects.
5. **Platforms are under-integrated into scene theory.** YouTube, Facebook, TikTok, Spotify, and messaging groups may reorganize locality, discovery, fandom, and genre boundaries.
6. **Behavioral-risk research is geographically skewed.** Music/substance studies rely heavily on European and North American adolescents and on genre labels that may not travel well.
7. **Selection and socialization remain difficult to separate.** Bangladesh-specific longitudinal network evidence is absent.
8. **Gender and class are not secondary variables.** Participation in the documented metal scene depended on time, money, instruments, venues, language skills, and male-dominated networks; comparable evidence for women and marginalized groups is limited.
9. **Political and religious identity need integration with youth-culture research.** Heritage-nationalist scholarship and contemporary scene scholarship rarely speak to each other.
10. **The rock/pop binary is analytically useful but empirically porous.** Hybrid genres, fan overlap, commercial crossover, and playlist-based listening require measurement rather than assumption.

## 6. Proposed research questions

### Review-paper questions

**RQ1.** Through what social and spatial mechanisms does music function as both a reflection and a producer of cultural and geographical identity?

**RQ2.** How do rock/metal and pop affiliations differ, and overlap, in their organization of authenticity, belonging, resistance, commerciality, and transnational connection?

**RQ3.** How do age, cohort, life stage, peer networks, and media environments shape musical identification?

**RQ4.** What does the evidence support about relationships among musical preference, peer groups, media exposure, and substance use, and where do causal claims exceed the designs?

**RQ5.** How does Bangladesh extend or challenge theories largely developed in Western settings?

### Proposed empirical-study questions

**ERQ1.** How do Bangladeshi participants across age cohorts narrate the role of music in personal, local, national, and transnational identity?

**ERQ2.** How do rock/metal and pop participants construct and police boundaries around authenticity, class, gender, language, religion, and commercial success?

**ERQ3.** How are musical preferences and friendship networks associated over time among Bangladeshi adolescents and young adults?

**ERQ4.** Are associations between musical affiliation and substance use attenuated after accounting for peer behavior, sensation seeking, family monitoring, socioeconomic position, nightlife participation, and media exposure?

**ERQ5.** How do platform use and city or region modify these identity and behavioral associations?

## 7. Methodology blueprint

### 7.1 Current paper: integrative review

The initial manuscript should be written as a structured integrative review with a focused Bangladesh case synthesis. It should not present new participant data, prevalence estimates, interview quotations, or causal results. The methods section should report the search strategy above, disciplinary breadth, inclusion logic, verification method, and limits. Findings should be organized thematically, not as if all studies estimate the same construct.

Recommended synthesis dimensions are: unit of identity; geographic scale; genre; age/life stage; social mechanism; method; key finding; causal strength; transferability to Bangladesh; and limitations. Cultural studies evidence should be evaluated for interpretive depth and reflexivity, while behavioral studies should be evaluated for temporality, confounding, measurement validity, and network design. A single biomedical hierarchy would misgrade ethnography.

### 7.2 Proposed empirical study: sequential mixed methods

The manuscript may conclude with a proposed empirical program, clearly labeled as future research.

**Phase A: Multisite qualitative mapping.** Conduct semi-structured interviews and participant observation in Dhaka plus at least two contrasting sites, ideally one secondary city with a visible music scene and one less metropolitan setting. Purposive maximum-variation sampling should include rock/metal musicians and fans, mainstream and independent pop participants, folk/heritage practitioners where relevant, K-pop or other transnational fandoms, promoters, venue workers, and listeners with weak genre identification. Sampling should deliberately balance gender and broaden class position.

**Phase B: Survey and ego-network study.** Recruit adolescents and young adults through schools, universities, workplaces, community settings, and online fan communities rather than only venues. Measure genre and artist preferences, strength and function of musical identification, identity scales, peer-network nominations, perceived and reported peer behavior, media/platform exposure, socioeconomic position, family monitoring, sensation seeking, religiosity, urbanicity, and substance-use outcomes. Use validated Bangla translations with cognitive interviewing and measurement-invariance testing.

**Phase C: Longitudinal network panel.** At least three waves are preferable for distinguishing friendship selection from peer influence. Stochastic actor-oriented models or comparable longitudinal network methods can test whether similarity in musical taste predicts tie formation and whether friends’ behaviors predict later change, while acknowledging residual confounding.

**Phase D: Comparative interpretation.** Compare Bangladeshi mechanisms with selected cases such as Hong Kong, Singapore, European adolescent studies, and diaspora research. The comparison should be mechanism-centered, not a country ranking.

### 7.3 Ethics

Research involving minors, substance use, political expression, religion, or stigmatized scenes requires formal review by the relevant institution before recruitment. Use assent and guardian consent where required, protect network data against deductive disclosure, separate identifiers from responses, avoid collecting unnecessary illegal-behavior detail, and provide local support information where surveys may cause distress. Participants should not be framed as risky because of genre affiliation. No human-subjects activity has been conducted for the current review.

### 7.4 Integration of the author's prior repository research

Two public repositories owned by the author were inspected on their default branches: `smile-plzz/bangladesh-music-evolution` and `smile-plzz/listener-taste-growth-profile`. Their drafts, research notes, annotated bibliography, and integrity report were treated as secondary research artifacts, not independent scholarly authorities. They materially improved discovery in four areas:

1. They identified Quader's companion study on forms of capital in the Dhaka metal scene, which sharpens the paper's class, gender, and access analysis.
2. They surfaced Bangladesh-specific work on folk-to-pop continuity, fusion, hip-hop activism, and industry/platform change. Only sources independently verified in this pass enter the core bibliography; unresolved records remain leads.
3. They supplied a stronger age/life-course evidence trail, especially the large cross-sectional study by Bonneville-Roussy and colleagues and the Music Preferences in Adulthood Model.
4. Their own audit documents exposed useful cautions: age is frequently confounded with cohort; popular industry statistics often have weak provenance; theoretical or derived audience-age profiles must not be reported as measured findings; and AI-assisted bibliographies require live record checks.

Repository-derived claims are not cited as findings in the paper. When prior repository work proposed an original model or interpretation, it is retained only as a candidate hypothesis until supported by published evidence or new empirical data.

## 9. Limitations of this research stage

- The search was broad and reproducible at the query level but was not run inside subscription databases such as Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, or RILM.
- No second independent reviewer screened records or extracted data.
- Full text was unavailable for some retained sources, so claims from those works were bounded to publisher abstracts and metadata.
- English-language indexing privileges Anglophone scholarship; Bangla-language research is likely underrepresented.
- “Rock,” “metal,” “pop,” “urban,” and “dance” are historically and nationally unstable categories.
- The Bangladesh corpus is too small and heterogeneous to support prevalence estimates or causal claims.
- Publication bias and the omission of theses, local journals, and archival sources may distort the map.
- Search results current to 11 October 2026 may change as new work is indexed or corrected.
- Prior AI-assisted repository research improved recall but may carry inherited errors. Its sources were triaged individually; unresolved items were not promoted into the verified corpus.

## 10. Research audit

### 10.1 Claim discipline

- No interviews, survey results, or Bangladesh prevalence figures were invented.
- No result from the proposed empirical study is written as an observed finding.
- Substance-use claims are described as associations, mediations, exposures, or hypotheses according to design.
- Content analyses are not treated as evidence that exposure changes behavior.
- Bangladesh-specific generalizations are bounded by the narrow available corpus.

### 10.2 Citation verification status

All references below have a verified DOI or stable scholarly publisher record. DOI resolution or publisher metadata was checked during this research stage. One edited scholarly book uses a stable JSTOR DOI. The bibliography excludes community forums and tertiary summaries encountered during discovery.

### 10.3 Evidence risks requiring attention during drafting

1. Avoid universal claims about what rock or pop “means.”
2. Do not collapse Bengali identity, Bangladeshi national identity, religious identity, and urban youth identity.
3. Distinguish Bangladesh from West Bengal while explaining their linked linguistic-cultural field.
4. Do not infer current scene conditions from the 2012 fieldwork reported by Quader and Redden.
5. Do not use genre preference as a proxy for substance use or risk status.
6. Keep cohort, age, and period analytically separate.
7. Mark recent Bangladesh studies with limited validation as emerging evidence rather than settled consensus.

### 10.4 AI assistance disclosure for the eventual paper

The manuscript should disclose that generative AI assisted with search-term development, evidence organization, and drafting. The human author remains responsible for checking every source, interpreting the evidence, revising the argument, and complying with the target venue’s policy. AI assistance must not be listed as authorship.

### 10.5 Conflict, protest, and peacebuilding expansion audit

The Stage 2 draft was expanded on 11 October 2026 to address music in mass protest, war, propaganda, incitement, collective memory, and peacebuilding. Additional search terms included *protest music*, *political mobilization*, *war songs*, *propaganda*, *incitement*, *hate music*, *violence*, *conflict transformation*, and *peacebuilding*. The added comparative cases cover Rwanda, Burma/Myanmar, white-power music, Israeli soldiers’ informal war songs, Northern Uganda, Afghanistan, Sri Lanka, and the Bangladesh Liberation War.

The expansion applies four claim controls:

1. Political expression is not treated as proof of mobilization.
2. Violent lyrical content is not treated as proof of violent behavioral effect.
3. Music is analyzed together with prior grievance, authoritative endorsement, organizational networks, media reach, coercive institutions, and opportunity for action.
4. Peacebuilding is treated as a contingent relational practice, not as an automatic consequence of shared performance.

Nine scholarly works were added to the manuscript bibliography for this expansion. Eight carry verified DOI records. The 2019 Swadhin Bangla Betar Kendra article was verified through the journal’s full-issue PDF and is retained despite the absence of a DOI because it provides directly relevant interviews and textual analysis. No conflict event, audience effect, interview, or causal result was invented.
